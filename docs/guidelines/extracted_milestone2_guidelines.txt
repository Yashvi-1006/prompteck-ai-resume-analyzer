1. Business Context and Opportunity Analysis
Recruitment, at its core, is a matching problem. A company defines what it needs; thousands of applicants claim they can deliver it. The breakdown happens in the middle — someone has to actually compare the two, and right now, that someone is an overworked HR executive staring at a spreadsheet of 400 names on a Monday morning.
The problem I kept coming back to when scoping this project was not that companies lack candidates. If anything, platforms like Naukri and LinkedIn have made the supply of applicants overwhelming. The real bottleneck is evaluation speed. Research from Ladders Inc. (2018) used eye-tracking to show that recruiters spend an average of 7.4 seconds on an initial resume scan. That is not a figure I found surprising — it is a figure I found disturbing, because it means most hiring decisions at the filtering stage are closer to a gut reaction than a reasoned judgment.
This is where the opportunity sits. Not in replacing the recruiter, but in doing the tedious pre-work for them. If a system can surface the top 10 genuinely relevant profiles from a pile of 300, the recruiter gets to do the part of their job that actually requires human judgment — interviewing, assessing culture fit, checking references. My project attempts to build exactly that layer: a semantic relevance engine that sits between application submission and human review.
2. Feasibility Study
I ran through three lenses when deciding whether this was actually buildable within the scope of a final-year project.
On the technical side, the answer was straightforwardly yes. The core functionality — parsing resumes, extracting entities, comparing them against a job description — is well-supported by existing Python libraries. spaCy handles named entity recognition with reasonable accuracy on professional text. For similarity scoring, Cosine Similarity via Scikit-learn works on TF-IDF vectors and does not require GPU infrastructure, which matters because I am building this on a standard laptop, not a cloud cluster. The honest limitation here is that TF-IDF misses synonyms — a resume that says "constructed" where the JD says "built" might score lower than it deserves. I plan to address this in a later version using sentence transformers, but for the current scope it is an acceptable tradeoff.
Operationally, the workflow I have designed is intentionally minimal: upload a JD, upload a folder of resumes, receive a ranked CSV. I made a deliberate choice not to build a complex dashboard at this stage because the core value is in the ranking logic, not the interface. A simpler interface also means fewer failure points during the demonstration.
The ethical dimension gave me the most pause. Any system that scores people carries the risk of encoding bias. My current approach strips out fields that are not relevant to job performance before the vectorisation step — names, photos, and age indicators are excluded from the parsed text. This is not a perfect solution, but it is a meaningful starting point. If this were to go into production, it would need a bias audit on its training data before deployment.
3. Monetisation and Revenue Model
I want to be upfront that this section is speculative — I am a student, not a startup founder, and these are possibilities I explored rather than validated business cases.
The most natural commercial path for this system is a B2B subscription aimed at small recruitment agencies and growing startups. These are organisations that receive enough applications to have a real screening problem but cannot afford enterprise ATS software like Workday or Greenhouse. A flat monthly fee in the ₹3,000–₹8,000 range per seat feels realistic for this segment, though that number would need market testing.
A second possibility came from a different direction entirely. While building this, I realised that job seekers face the mirror image of the same problem — they do not know why their resume keeps getting filtered out. A B2C tool that lets a student upload their resume against a specific JD and receive a match score plus actionable suggestions could work on a freemium basis. The free tier gives a score; a paid tier gives specific gap analysis. The conversion rate would likely be low, but the user volume in a market like India could make it viable.
The third option — licensing the ranking logic as an API to existing job portals — is probably the highest-upside but lowest-probability path for something at this stage. It would require the algorithm to be significantly more robust and independently audited before any serious platform would integrate it. I include it here as a long-term direction rather than a near-term plan.

Patent Applicability
I will be straight about this — no, I do not think this can be patented right now. And I think it is more honest to explain that properly than to dress it up.
The thing is, nothing I built is technically new. TF-IDF, Cosine Similarity, Named Entity Recognition — these are all standard tools that have been in textbooks for years. I did not invent any of them. I combined them in a way that made sense for resume screening, but combining existing methods is not the same as inventing something. LinkedIn and Naukri have been doing some version of resume matching for over a decade. I am not doing anything they have not already done in some form.
I did look into what actually qualifies for a patent in India. Under the Patents Act 1970 there are three things it needs — novelty, an inventive step, and industrial applicability. My project probably clears the last one. But the inventive step condition is where I genuinely cannot make a strong case. The penalty factor for senior roles and the must-have versus nice-to-have skill tiers — those were my own decisions and I think they are sensible ones — but any developer working on the same problem would likely arrive at something similar. That is not invention, that is just logical design.
There is also a section of Indian patent law — Section 3(k) — that specifically excludes computer programs and mathematical methods from being patented on their own. A resume ranking algorithm is dangerously close to exactly that. I asked my dad about this actually since he has some exposure to legal and compliance stuff at work and he said the same thing — software patents in India are tricky and you need to show a very specific technical effect that goes beyond just running code on a machine.
So where does that leave me. Honestly, the current version is not patent material. But I do think there is a future version that could be different — specifically if I built a model that actually learns from recruiter decisions over time and adapts its scoring based on real hiring outcomes. That kind of adaptive feedback loop does not exist in what I have built yet and might be genuinely novel enough to be worth looking into. If I ever get there I would file a provisional application with the Indian Patent Office just to lock in the date, and figure out the full application from there.
For now though I am more focused on finishing what I started than filing for something I cannot honestly defend yet.
